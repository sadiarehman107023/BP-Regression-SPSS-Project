**Blood Pressure Regression Analysis Project Summary**
***Project Title: Predictors of Systolic Blood Pressure: A Multiple Linear Regression Analysis***
Research Question: To what extent do demographic factors (age, gender) and lifestyle variables (BMI, physical activity, salt intake, smoking status, and stress level) simultaneously predict systolic blood pressure, and does smoking status moderate the relationship between age and blood pressure?Dataset Description: The dataset consists of N = 180 entries tracking physiological and lifestyle metrics. Data screening revealed a minimal missing data rate (~3%), specifically missing 6 cases for BMI and 4 cases for stress level. The final analytical sample following automatic listwise deletion consists of N = 174 participants.Methods Used:Data Screening: Evaluation of minimum/maximum values for data integrity and Shapiro-Wilk testing for dependent variable normality.Bivariate Correlation: Pearson's r correlation matrix to pre-check multicollinearity and evaluate isolated linear relationships.Multiple Linear Regression: Simultaneous ("Enter" method) ordinary least squares (OLS) regression with 7 predictors, paired with residual diagnostics (Durbin-Watson, standardized residual limits, and P-P plots) to verify regression assumptions.Moderation Analysis: Computation and evaluation of an interaction term (age_x_smoking) via an 8-predictor regression model.Key Findings:The core 7-predictor model was highly statistically significant, F(7, 166) = 60.15, p < .001, explaining 71.7% of the variance in systolic blood pressure (Adjusted R² = .705).Age emerged as the strongest positive predictor of blood pressure (r = .584), closely followed by BMI (r = .476). Salt intake and smoking status also behaved as significant risk factors.Physical activity acted as a statistically significant protective factor (r = -.214), proving that increased exercise correlates with lower blood pressure.Stress level and gender were non-significant predictors in this model, showcasing a realistic clinical scenario where a hypothesized variable (stress) does not show a significant effect.The moderation analysis confirmed that the interaction term was not statistically significant (p = .146), meaning the baseline risk of age increasing blood pressure remains steady regardless of whether an individual smokes.Technical Report1. Data Screening & Assumption TestingPrior to conducting the primary analysis, data screening was performed on all variables. Visual inspection of minimum and maximum values revealed no implausible data points or data entry errors. Evaluation of missing data indicated that 6 cases were missing for body mass index (BMI) and 4 cases were missing for stress level (~3% of the total sample). Because missingness was minimal and assumed to be Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), listwise deletion was employed automatically during the regression analysis, resulting in a final analytical sample of N = 174.The assumption of normality for the dependent variable, systolic blood pressure (SBP), was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The test indicated that the distribution did not significantly deviate from normality, W = 0.98, p = .058, and visual inspection of the histogram confirmed a generally symmetrical, bell-shaped distribution. Pearson bivariate correlations were conducted as a multicollinearity pre-check; no two predictor variables correlated above the critical threshold of r = .70, with the strongest predictor-to-predictor relationship occurring between BMI and physical activity (r = -.21, p = .006).2. Regression Model EvaluationA multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine how well age, BMI, physical activity, salt intake, smoking status, stress level, and gender simultaneously predict systolic blood pressure. The overall model was highly statistically significant, F(7, 166) = 60.15, p < .001, and explained 71.7% of the total variance in systolic blood pressure (Adjusted R² = .705).The Durbin-Watson statistic was 2.05, demonstrating that the assumption of independent errors was fully met. Residual diagnostics revealed a well-behaved model, with standardized residuals falling safely between a minimum of -2.33 and a maximum of 3.22, indicating no severe multivariate outliers.3. Exploratory Moderation AnalysisFinally, an exploratory moderation analysis was executed to test whether the relationship between age and systolic blood pressure was altered by smoking status. An interaction term (age × smoking status) was computed and added to the regression model.The interaction effect was not statistically significant, B = 0.14, β = .22, t(165) = 1.46, p = .146, 95% CI [-0.05, 0.32], and its inclusion introduced substantial multicollinearity into the model (VIF = 12.85). This indicates that the effect of age on systolic blood pressure remains stable regardless of whether an individual smokes or not.
